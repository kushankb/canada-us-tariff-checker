Americans mostly buy toilet paper made in America. The pulp it's made from
comes from Canada, and since 22 August that pulp is taxed 50% on the way in.

The finished rolls aren't on the tariff list. The raw material is.

I built a search tool for the Canada–US tariffs, mainly because everything else
I found assumed you already knew your six-digit customs code. This one lets you
type "toilet paper" or "sweater" and tells you whether it's caught and at what
rate. It covers all 648 lines on Canada's counter-tariff list and all 554 on the
US Section 338 list.

Two things surprised me while putting it together.

Being under the duty-free threshold doesn't save you. Canada's surtax applies
to small parcels that would normally owe nothing at all, and it applies even
where the postal and courier remission orders would otherwise give relief.
Carrying something back yourself is a different story — the traveller's
exemption does hold.

And the tariffs stack. A drinking glass coming into the US already paid 7.2% to
get in. The new 50% doesn't replace that, it gets added to it. So 57.2%, before
anything else.

Everything in it comes straight from Finance Canada's published list and the US
tariff schedule, and every code is checked back against the source before it
goes in. Nothing is estimated.

## Notes before you post

- The 50% on pulp is real and checkable: tariff lines 4803.00.40 (tissue stock)
  and 4702.00.00 (woodpulp) are both named in US note 51. Finished toilet paper,
  4818.10, is not.
- The 7.2% is the published ordinary duty on 7013.99.90.
- If you want the Procter & Gamble angle in the post, attribute it: the Guardian
  reported it, on World Bank trade figures. Don't state it flat.
- The tool is not customs advice and the post shouldn't imply otherwise. "Have a
  look" is the right ask; "use this to classify your shipment" is not.
